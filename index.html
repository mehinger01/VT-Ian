Session 2: Dashboard Activation and School-System Stress Test

Follow the steps in order. Each section includes exactly what to say, what Ian should do, what to observe, and what to record.

Step 1

Accountability check and reset

Determine whether the five-minute start transferred outside tutoring.

0–7 min
Say: “Last time, your challenge was to use the five-minute start on the laundry. Walk me through exactly what happened—not what was supposed to happen.”
Diagnose precisely:
Forgot = capture failure.
Remembered but delayed = initiation failure.
Started but stopped = persistence failure.
Completed = strategy transferred.
Success marker: Ian can explain that action can come before motivation.
Step 2

Expose the current decision process

Let Ian solve the problem before teaching the system.

7–12 min

Opening scenario

It is Tuesday at 4:15 p.m. You have Algebra II problems, Chemistry reading, an English essay due next week, a History assignment due tomorrow, and an Engineering project that has not been started. Your school platform also shows several notifications. What do you do first?

Relies on memory
Chooses the easiest task first
Uses the nearest deadline only
Treats large assignments as one vague task
Does not check all assignment sources
Say: “The goal is not to remember everything. The goal is to build a system that remembers for you.”
Teach the cycle: Capture → Clarify → Prioritize → Start → Recover → Close
Step 3

Capture a realistic workload

Ian enters tasks into the command center. Do not enter them for him.

12–23 min
Rule: If it is not captured, it does not exist.

Open the Command Center tab and use the preloaded junior-year workload. Require complete information for each task.

Algebra II

18 practice problems; correct a quiz; Friday quiz.

Chemistry

Read Section 1.2; lab analysis; 12 vocabulary terms; unit quiz.

Honors English

Read 25 pages; annotate; develop a thesis; begin literary analysis.

Honors History

Read a section; take notes; answer four document questions.

Engineering

Create a design sketch; update project log; prepare materials.

Required fields

Course, task, due date, estimate, priority, visible next action, strategy, status, return point.

Ask repeatedly: “Could you begin this task from what you wrote, without asking another question?”
Step 4

Clarify visible next actions

Convert categories into actions that can be physically observed.

23–31 min
Too vague:
Study Chemistry
Work on essay
Do History
Start project
Visible actions:
Answer five vocabulary questions without notes.
Write one possible thesis.
Open to page 84 and read the heading.
Sketch two possible designs.
Say: “A next action passes one test: could I watch you do it?”
Step 5

Prioritize by consequence, not comfort

Ian ranks the workload and defends his top three choices.

31–39 min
Priority 1

Do first

Due soon, high consequence, blocks other work, or requires strong focus.

Priority 2

Start early

Not due immediately, but large enough to become a crisis if delayed.

Priority 3

Quick complete

Short, clear, ready to finish—but not automatically first.

Priority 4

Monitor

Later deadline; remains visible and receives a future action date.

Ask: “What must be completed today? What must be started today? Which task becomes dangerous if ignored?”
Step 6

Estimate real time

Replace ideal-time guesses with complete-task estimates.

39–45 min
Formula: Setup + Work + Check + Submit = Real Time
Ask: “What part did your original estimate leave out?”
Step 7

Choose a start strategy and launch

Ian chooses the method based on the task, then begins immediately.

45–51 min

Five-minute start

Use for avoidance, overwhelm, or waiting for motivation.

Focused work block

Use for sustained work with a defined target.

Quick complete

Use only for truly brief tasks with materials ready.

Multi-day breakdown

Use for essays, tests, projects, and multi-stage assignments.

05:00
Observe: Does Ian begin, or does he keep planning? Does he know the exact first action? Does he need redirection?
Step 8

Practice distraction recovery

The goal is fast return, not perfect uninterrupted focus.

51–55 min
Recovery routine: Notice → Name → Reset → Resume
Ian's script: “I was pulled away by ____. My next action is ____. I will work for ____ minutes.”
Step 9

Close the loop

Nothing unfinished is allowed to disappear back into memory.

55–57 min
Is it finished?
Was it checked?
Was it submitted?
If unfinished, does it have an exact return point?
Say: “Unfinished work must have a return point.”
Final stress test

Rebuild the plan when conditions change

Ian must operate the system under pressure.

57–60 min

New information

It is 4:15 p.m. You have a commitment at 6:30. The Chemistry quiz moves one day earlier. English reading took longer than expected. An Engineering assignment was announced verbally in class.

Require Ian to: capture the new task, update the deadline, reorder priorities, revise estimates, choose strategies, and create return points.

Ian's Multi-Session Arc

The sequence reflects the first-session findings and keeps executive functioning tied to real academic performance.

1

Baseline and immediate learning systems — completed

Before–during–after class, Pomodoro, five-minute start, assessments, and immediate application.

Outcome: strong engagement and a clear map of consistency gaps.

Dashboard activation and school-system stress test

Capture, clarify, prioritize, estimate, start, recover, and close.

Outcome: one functioning school-management system.

Math and science test performance

Spaced retrieval, mixed practice, vocabulary recall, error analysis, pacing, and checking.

Prompts, claims, evidence, outlines, staged drafts, rubrics, and multi-day planning.

Outcome: large assignments become controlled sequences.

Independent performance across a full workload

Combine planning, class preparation, reading, notes, test review, projects, and unexpected changes.

Outcome: determine which systems are becoming independent habits.
