The Origins of Lacrosse — Part 1: Before the Modern Sport

Long before lacrosse became a school, college, professional, or international sport, Indigenous peoples across North America were playing many forms of stick-and-ball games. These games were not identical, and it is misleading to imagine that there was one original version with a single set of rules. Communities developed their own traditions, field sizes, equipment, and purposes. Among the Haudenosaunee peoples of the northeastern part of the continent, the game carries deep cultural and spiritual meaning. It is often described as a gift from the Creator and is connected to community identity, ceremony, and responsibility as well as athletic competition.

Early games could look dramatically different from modern lacrosse. A match might include dozens or even hundreds of players. Instead of a carefully measured field with painted boundaries, play could stretch across a large open area, and natural landmarks might help define the goals. Games could last for many hours. The sticks themselves were made from wood, with pockets woven from rawhide or other natural materials, and the ball could be made from animal hide. Because each nation had its own traditions, the exact rules, number of players, and style of play varied.

The purposes of the game also varied. It could be played for recreation, to build endurance and toughness, to strengthen relationships within or between communities, or as part of ceremonial and spiritual life. In some settings, games were associated with healing, preparation, diplomacy, or the settling of disputes. That mixture of purposes is important because it challenges a modern assumption: today people often separate sports from religion, politics, community identity, and diplomacy, but Indigenous games did not necessarily fit into those modern categories.

When Europeans encountered these games, they were seeing a tradition that was already old. In the seventeenth century, French missionaries in what is now Canada wrote about Indigenous stick-and-ball games. The Jesuit missionary Jean de Brébeuf described a game he observed among the Wendat, whom French sources called the Huron, in 1636. Europeans eventually began using the French term “lacrosse” for the game. That European name, however, did not mark the beginning of the sport. It marked the beginning of a written European record of a much older Indigenous tradition.

This distinction matters when historians ask where a sport “began.” Written records often survive more easily than oral traditions, ceremonies, or community knowledge. If historians begin the story only when Europeans wrote something down, they can accidentally make earlier Indigenous history appear invisible. A more accurate history of lacrosse therefore begins before standardized rules, before organized clubs, and before the word “lacrosse” itself became common.

The Origins of Lacrosse — Part 2: From Indigenous Game to Codified Sport

By the nineteenth century, non-Indigenous players in Canada were increasingly adopting the game. This was a major turning point. Indigenous communities continued to play their own forms of lacrosse, but clubs created by non-Indigenous players wanted rules that would make games more predictable between teams. In 1856, the Montreal Lacrosse Club was founded. A Canadian dentist and lacrosse enthusiast named William George Beers became influential in developing a standardized version of the sport. During the 1860s, written rules helped establish clearer expectations about the number of players, the size of the field, the equipment, and how games would be played.

Standardization helped lacrosse spread. When teams in different places use roughly the same rules, they can compete more easily. Clubs formed, spectators could follow the action, and the sport moved into schools and organized athletic programs. By the late nineteenth century, lacrosse was being played beyond Canada and the United States, including in Britain and Australia. It later appeared in the Olympic Games in 1904 and 1908. In that sense, codification helped transform a family of Indigenous games into an international organized sport.

But the transformation created a historical problem as well. The easier it became to identify a club, a printed rulebook, or an official competition, the easier it became to tell the story as though modern lacrosse had been created by the people who standardized it. That version confuses codification with invention. Beers and other organizers helped shape the rules of modern field lacrosse, but they did not create the underlying game from nothing. The sport they organized grew from Indigenous traditions that had existed for centuries.

The story is also more complicated than a simple handoff from Indigenous people to non-Indigenous athletes. Indigenous players continued to shape the sport after formal rules appeared. Haudenosaunee communities maintained lacrosse as a living cultural tradition rather than a relic from the past. Stick making, community games, elite competition, and the spiritual meaning of the game continued across generations. In the modern era, the Haudenosaunee Nationals have competed internationally, representing the Haudenosaunee Confederacy rather than a conventional nation-state. Their presence on the world stage makes an important point: Indigenous people are not merely part of lacrosse’s distant origin story; they remain active participants in its present.

For a historian, lacrosse is therefore a useful case study in how history gets framed. One version might begin with Indigenous nations, cultural traditions, and centuries of play. Another might begin with nineteenth-century clubs and written rules. Both can contain true facts, but they do not tell the same story. The starting point changes who appears to be central.

A careful account holds both developments together. Indigenous peoples created and sustained the games from which lacrosse developed. Later, non-Indigenous clubs standardized particular versions, helping the sport spread through schools, clubs, international competition, and eventually the Olympics. Understanding both parts gives a fuller history: lacrosse is simultaneously an ancient Indigenous cultural tradition and a modern organized sport. The most accurate story does not erase either side, but it recognizes which came first and how the game changed as different groups adopted, reshaped, and carried it forward.

Fixed school structure

Class periods and the school day are mostly fixed, so the planner does not need to recreate every minute.

After-school capacity

Show sports, activities, appointments, dinner, travel, and the actual time left for schoolwork.

Due date + start date

Every meaningful task gets both. This prevents Friday projects from first becoming visible Thursday night.

Work blocks

Translate tasks into realistic ~20-minute blocks rather than vague “study time.”

Tests and retrieval

Place short retrieval sessions before tests instead of one large cram block.

Tonight strip

The planner should always answer: What are the 2–4 things that must happen tonight?
